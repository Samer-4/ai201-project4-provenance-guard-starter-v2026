## What This Does

Provenance Guard is a Flask API that assesses whether writing exhibits patterns associated with AI-generated text. It is a heuristic detector, not proof of authorship.

1. A writer submits `text` and `creator_id` to `POST /submit`.
2. The API validates both inputs and generates a unique `content_id`.
3. `detector.py` calculates a GPT-2-based predictability signal.
4. `stylometry.py` measures sentence-length variation, vocabulary diversity, and punctuation density.
5. `scoring.py::combine_signals` combines both signals with equal weights.
6. The combined score determines the `human`, `unsure`, or `ai` classification and a human-readable explanation.
7. The decision is recorded in an append-only audit log. `POST /appeal` lets a writer request review, creating an `under_review` event while preserving the original decision.

---

## Signals and Scoring

### Signal one — the model signal

**What it measures:** GPT-2-based text predictability, transformed into a score from 0 to 1. Higher values indicate more AI-like patterns according to this heuristic.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** Generated writing can contain predictable wording and conventional sentence structures.

**What it can't see:** Predictability does not establish authorship. Human writers using simple vocabulary, including non-native speakers, may be falsely flagged. Unusual AI-generated writing may evade detection. The score is not a calibrated probability.

### Signal two — the style signal

**What it measures:** Sentence-length spread, type-token ratio (vocabulary diversity), and punctuation density.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** Repetitive structure and vocabulary can sometimes be associated with machine-generated writing.

**What it can't see:** Stylometry does not understand meaning or authorship. Formal human writing can appear repetitive, while minor edits can change the score without changing the author.

### The combining rule

**The rule:** Both signals receive equal weight. If they disagree, neither overrides the other; their average determines the final score.

**The numbers:** `combined_score = round(0.5 * model_score + 0.5 * style_score, 4)`. Scores below 0.35 are classified as human; scores above 0.65 are classified as AI; scores from 0.35 to 0.65, inclusive, are unsure.

**Where it lives:** `scoring.py::combine_signals`

**A case where my two signals split, and what my rule does with it:**

For the sample submission, the model signal was `0.406` and the style signal was `0.5733`. Their combined score was `0.4897`, resulting in `unsure`. The disagreement illustrates why combining signals and retaining an uncertain classification is important.

---

## Label Variants

| Label | Score range | The exact text a reader sees |
|---|---|---|
| high-confidence human | Below 0.35 | This writing shows patterns associated with human writing, but authorship cannot be confirmed. |
| unsure | 0.35 through 0.65, inclusive | There is not enough evidence to determine whether this writing was generated by AI or written by a person. |
| high-confidence AI | Above 0.65 | This writing shows patterns associated with AI-generated text, but the result is not definitive. |

**Why I worded the "unsure" one this way:** The wording acknowledges insufficient evidence without accusing a writer of misconduct. Since both signals have limitations, uncertainty should be communicated explicitly.

The `confidence` field is calculated as `round(abs(combined_score - 0.5) * 2, 4)`. It measures distance from the neutral midpoint, not a calibrated probability of correctness.
